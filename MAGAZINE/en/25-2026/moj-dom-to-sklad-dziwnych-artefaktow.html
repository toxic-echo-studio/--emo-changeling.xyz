My Home Is Basically the Infydel Stash - On Stolen Toy Trauma, Neurodivergence, and Goblin Mode

If an average neurotypical normie walked straight off the street into my room or down into our basement, their brain would probably suffer an instantaneous blue screen. Through the eyes of someone raised on sterile flat-pack furniture catalogs and minimalist decluttering tutorials, my personal space looks like an unmitigated disaster zone. Total, unchecked chaos. Towering stacks of cardboard boxes, drawers stuffed to the brim with bizarre loot, antique biscuit tins harboring junk no sane person would deem valuable, worn-out relics, rocks, and biological leftovers. The immediate, knee-jerk reaction from the outside world is always the same tired refrain: "Dude, just throw all this trash away, why are you even hoarding it?". But beneath this apparent mess, behind this unfiltered goblin mode, lies zero mindless hoarding. What actually fuels it is a very specific, deeply rooted psychology, the reality of neurodivergence, and an agonizing, unresolved childhood trauma that the neurotypical world remains completely blind to.

Let's lay down the foundational thesis right away, because it is the exact engine that holds this entire universe together: autistic people, folks with ADHD, and plural systems navigating dissociation (DID / plurality) bond with physical objects through a fundamentally different emotional blueprint than the rest of society. The neurotypical mainstream defines love and respect for a toy or object strictly through active, physical wear-and-tear. A kid is expected to drag their stuffed animal through the dirt, rip the doll's hair out, crash the toy car into walls, and grind those belongings into dust until they physically disintegrate. Only then do adults nod approvingly and claim the toy "served its purpose". For a neurodivergent kid, reality operates in the exact opposite direction: the ultimate form of respect toward a cherished object is PROTECTION.

Whenever I got my hands on something deeply meaningful as a kid, I never threw it around or battered it across the floor. If it happened to be a fancier doll, it stayed right inside its original packaging, kept in mint factory condition so no dust or damage could ever touch it. When I collected Littlest Pet Shop figurines, they sat proudly on their dedicated display shelves or nestled inside their little house, standing strictly at attention under a sacred, non-negotiable rule: nobody was allowed to lay a finger on them. My herd of model horses had their own secure sanctuary perched on top of a wardrobe. To me, the sheer joy of ownership lived entirely in the fact that these items were kept safe and intact. Once in a blue moon, I would open a box, slide the treasure out, admire every meticulous sculpted detail, soak in its presence, gently wipe away any speck of dust, and place it right back into its haven. That tiny, controlled sanctuary was my sensory anchor against a loud, hostile world that overwhelmed me at every turn.

And then, with the full-blown entitlement of neurotypical adulthood, the caregiver barges into that sanctuary. In my case, it was my great-grandmother, whom I lived with at the time. A person from an entirely different era, viewing existence purely through the lens of cold, mechanical utility. An adult like that glances at a sealed box sitting high on a shelf and runs a crude, dismissive calculation: "They don't play with it, they don't drag it out every day, it's just sitting there collecting dust, so they clearly don't need it." And that is where the quiet, sickening routine began: regular, systematic theft. Behind my back, while I was away at school or out of the house, my most treasured, lovingly protected possessions were stolen and handed out like cheap party favors to younger brats in the extended family. Because, after all, "they will actually play with them."

Very few things in this world break you quite like rushing back to your secret stash, opening your favorite box, and staring into total, hollow emptiness. It was completely gone. And weeks later, during some suffocating family dinner, you were forced to sit there and watch your sacred, untouchable treasure being brutalized, snapped in half, filthy, slobbered on, and trampled across the floor by a screaming kid who treated it like disposable trash. And you were not even allowed to make a sound. If you dared to object, if you screamed that it was your property and that it had been stolen from you, the full weight of adult gaslighting came crashing down on your head. The entire family closed ranks to shame and crush you: "Stop being so selfish," "you are acting crazy," "they are just stupid toys anyway," and that quintessential, authoritarian classic: "children don't own anything."

Right then and there, something vital permanently snapped inside me. You learn the cold, ugly truth: your boundaries are nonexistent, your emotions are a joke to the adults around you, and anything you dare to love out in the open will be systematically ripped from your hands and destroyed under the excuse of "family harmony." That is the exact crucible where severe attachment disruption to physical objects is born. As an adult, your subconscious flips into permanent defensive lockdown. Every single item that triggers even the slightest spark of emotional resonance - whether it is a ridiculous charred fry, a piece of string, a dead gadget, or a twisted chunk of scrap metal - becomes an impenetrable fortress. It stays with you for good, it goes into the stash, and it is defended with goblin ferocity. Because these are not useless trinkets. They are tangible anchors of your autonomy that no adult, no thief, and no self-righteous authority figure will ever be allowed to touch again.

When you view our multi-story flat in this aging tenement building through that lens, it stops looking like an odd storage unit and reveals itself as a living ecosystem of intertwined lore. Those wooden floors, hiding expansive hollow spaces above ancient ceilings, are not merely historic architecture. They were once home to an active, parallel civilization of wild field mice. While an ordinary homeowner would immediately deploy snap traps, poisons, and exterminators, our household operated under entirely different ethics. Since we couldn't drive them out without causing harm, my roommate - who already had extensive history navigating wild rodents - established a simple philosophy: you either suffer, or you learn to live alongside them. The mice were granted their own quiet corner, clean water, and regular food. In return, they never chewed through a single cable, never raided the pantry cupboards, and caused zero mess. We bonded with them to the point where they would casually wander out into the center of the room just to check in on us.

That was the soil that birthed the legend of Xanax - a single wild field mouse who stood so far apart from the squabbling factions under the floorboards that we routinely joked about him being on the spectrum. He was extraordinarily peaceful. He accepted our presence without fear and began bringing food directly to my flatmate under his pillow. Imagine a tiny wild rodent, capable of carrying at most two kernels in its mouth at a time, sprinting back and forth across the flat all night long just to pile up dried corn and sunflower seeds under a human's head. He did it out of genuine, uncalculated herd loyalty: he decided we were part of his family, and he made sure his pack was fed. And when his final hour arrived, he didn't crawl away to die alone in the darkness of the walls. He used his last ounce of strength to drag himself out into the middle of the room, approaching us weak and weary, simply seeking comfort and presence in his final moments. The single, shriveled corn kernel left behind from his visits rests in my drawer today as a relic. To an outsider, it is kitchen compost. To me, it is living proof that a tiny wild creature possessed more genuine empathy, respect, and loyalty than the human beings who raised me. We still joke that Xanax crossed over into the astral plane, morphed into an overcaffeinated ghost lizard with a strip of fur down his back, and spends his afterlife escaping spectral enclosures - yet that humble piece of corn remains an unshakeable monument to that bond.

Every single one of these artifacts weaves into a narrative that needs no sterile catalog numbers, because each tin shouts its own unfiltered history. Pop open the mystery box watched over by the "googly-eyed guardian" - a leftover from the phase where I glued plastic eyes onto household appliances, declaring that anything with eyes possesses a soul, turning the entire apartment into a massive hive mind whose feelings we had to protect. Inside rests that legendary, carbonized french fry and a piece of raw pasta shaped like a rooster, handed to me ages ago as a totally absurd, unprompted gift. Right beside them lies a bag containing the "superior yeast" - an ancient, long-expired packet born from an inside joke about searching for a supreme, asexual organism whose biological simplicity effortlessly outshines all petty human melodrama.

Dig a little deeper, and the physical timeline of bodily dysphoria, alt aesthetics, and institutional trauma unfolds. A yellowed, vintage razor blade wrapper from the golden era before pre-styled synthetic wigs, when cutting razor-sharp scene bangs required standing over a sink with a bare double-edged blade, sits right next to scorchingly spicy instant ramen packets. A weathered metal autoclave container - hailing from the bygone days of medicine when surgical tools and heavy metal syringes were sterilized for reuse - houses an ensemble so surreal it belongs in a contemporary art biennial: a dried chestnut, a miniature booklet listing basic human rights under the European Union, and a pocket-sized Quran equipped with its own tiny reading loupe. It stands as an accidental, razor-sharp installation mocking the absurdity of grand legal codes and organized dogmas, resting peacefully side-by-side on a white lace doily inside vintage medical steel.

Right adjacent sits a pastel unicorn diary sealed with a tiny tin padlock. It looks like a harmless, sugary piece of stationery, yet it instantly strikes a raw nerve of deep school trauma. It drags back memories of relentless institutional force, when my nervous system was fried, when chronic illness made simply drawing breath an uphill battle, and the school system violently tortured me over handwriting and cursive drills. To this day, the sight of handwritten script triggers an immediate sensory recoil and defensive freeze. So the unicorn diary remains locked, pristine, and entirely unwritten - a silent middle finger to institutional coercion.

This home is also a shared territory with another human being, whose eclectic past constantly bleeds into my personal vaults. Shelves groan under stray munitions loot: spent shell casings, strange machine-pressed metal plates of unidentifiable origin that I snagged off their shelves simply because they looked too epic to leave behind. In the closet rests a heavy, rough-hewn hunting knife hand-forged by my roommate back when they were neck-deep in traditional blacksmithing, tucked in beside a ragtag fleet of battered folding pocket knives that survived their own trials by fire. Deeper in the wardrobes hide blunt steel fencing swords and an old felt dagger from early live-action roleplay campaigns - relics of an era when people vanished into the wilderness for days to build entire alternative realities out of pure passion, with zero cameras and zero algorithmic vanity.

In that same drawer lies a humble plastic claw clip salvaged from a hair salon where I briefly worked. Viewed from the side, its hinges and teeth form the face of a strange little one-eyed beast. During grueling, lonely shifts under late capitalism, I would prop it up on the break room table and talk to it like a miniature companion just to keep my sanity intact. Right next to it stands a plastic concert tumbler snagged from the Bring Me The Horizon show at Tauron Arena, carried out purely on principle - because when predatory mega-promoters charge extortionate fees for every breath you take, keeping the plastic cup you already paid a forced deposit on becomes a tiny, satisfying act of DIY defiance.

And finally, the most intimate layer: the intersection of identity, spirituality, and mortality. The taxidermy tins: a glass vial packed to the brim with tarantula fangs. A grim reminder of the time my fascination with arachnids led to two solid weeks of agony, with swollen, throbbing hands peppered with microscopic urticating hairs that burrowed into flesh like vicious fiberglass while sorting through molts. Pest-eaten rodent bones preserved in ziplock baggies, insect wings, and iridescent bird feathers. Further down lies a handmade leather pony halter - a gift from years ago tied directly to my Zebroid identity and deep appreciation for pony play aesthetics. To the closed-minded mainstream, it is merely cheap fodder for sleazy jokes and knee-jerk sexualization. To me, it represents masterful anatomical craft - an intricate discipline dedicated to sculpting the illusion of equine lines, hooves, and harness gear that enables genuine non-human self-expression without reducing everything to hollow adult entertainment. And resting at the very bottom is a smooth, cold pebble - the material anchor from a fierce energetic duel fought across the astral plane - alongside a scratched Nirvana CD won at age eleven on a forgotten MySpace-era Facebook community, accompanied by a handwritten letter from Helion.

Every single one of these items represents a distinct scar and a personal medal. When you look at someone operating in full goblin mode, drop the condescending sneer and stop asking why they haven't sent it all to the landfill. These seemingly useless odds and ends are our chitin armor. They form the one sanctuary where a neurodivergent kid who was routinely robbed and silenced can finally plant their boots on their own terms and declare: "This belongs to me. I decide what matters. And you will never take it away from me again."
